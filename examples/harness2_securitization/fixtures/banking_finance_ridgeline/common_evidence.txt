L18: **[Section 1: Introduction and Purpose]{.underline}**
L19: 
L20: This playbook provides standard negotiation positions for Whitfield & Crane attorneys representing borrowers---and, in some instances, sponsor-backed portfolio companies---in middle-market senior secured revolving credit facilities, typically ranging from \$50,000,000 to \$300,000,000 in committed amounts.
L21: 
L22: **Target Borrower Profile.** The guidance herein is calibrated for lower-middle-market and middle-market companies with revenues of \$100,000,000 to \$750,000,000 and EBITDA of \$25,000,000 to \$125,000,000. These borrowers are frequently sponsor-backed (private equity portfolio companies) and may be pursuing active acquisition strategies.
L23: 
L24: **Three-Tier Framework.** For each provision addressed below, the playbook identifies three tiers of negotiation positions:
L25: 
L26: 1\. **Preferred Position** --- the firm\'s recommended opening or optimal term;
L27: 
L28: 2\. **Acceptable Fallback** --- a position the firm considers commercially reasonable and within market norms if the Preferred Position cannot be achieved; and
L29: 
L30: 3\. **Hard No** --- a term or threshold that the firm recommends never accepting because it is materially below market or creates unacceptable risk for borrower clients.
L31: 
L32: **Scope Limitations.** This playbook does not cover every possible term sheet provision. Attorneys should apply independent judgment informed by deal-specific facts, current market conditions, and client priorities where the playbook is silent. In particular, certain topics---including **commitment fee structures** and **maturity extension mechanics**---are highly deal-specific and are not addressed in this playbook. Attorneys should consult with the lead partner on a case-by-case basis when these provisions arise in a markup.
L33: 
L34: **Market Data Sources.** The positions and market observations reflected in this playbook are based on the firm\'s experience across approximately 45 middle-market revolving credit facility transactions closed between January 2022 and September 2024, supplemented by publicly available market data from recognized industry sources.

L36: **[Section 2: Interest Rate and SOFR Floor Provisions]{.underline}**
L37: 
L38: **Context.** Virtually all middle-market revolving credit facilities now reference Term SOFR (plus credit spread adjustment, if applicable). SOFR floors have become increasingly common since 2022 but remain a negotiable point, particularly in bilateral and club revolving facilities.
L39: 
L40: **Preferred Position.** SOFR floor of 0.00%. The borrower should bear true floating-rate exposure without an embedded minimum reference rate that creates a hidden cost in declining-rate environments. A 0.00% floor means the borrower benefits fully from any rate decreases.
L41: 
L42: **Acceptable Fallback.** SOFR floor of no more than 0.25%. At 0.25%, the floor has minimal practical impact in most rate environments and is generally considered within market range for middle-market revolving facilities. A 0.25% floor begins to bite only when SOFR drops below 0.25%, which reflects an extremely accommodative monetary policy environment.
L43: 
L44: **Hard No.** SOFR floor above 0.50%. Any floor at or above 0.50% creates meaningful economic cost in a rate-cutting cycle. At current forward curve expectations (SOFR potentially declining to the 3.00%-3.50% range by late 2025), a floor above 0.50% has limited near-term impact but creates material tail risk. A floor of 0.75% or higher is above market for middle-market revolvers and should be resisted in all circumstances.
L45: 
L46: **Negotiation Tips.** Remind counterparty counsel that SOFR floors are more commonly associated with term loan B and broadly syndicated facilities; in bilateral or club revolving facilities, floors above 0.25% are less common. If the lender insists on a floor, propose reducing it to the 0.10%-0.25% range and document that the margin grid already compensates the lender for credit risk. A floor above 0.50% should not be accepted regardless of margin concessions offered in return.
L47: 
L48: **[Section 3: Applicable Margin and Pricing Grid Mechanics]{.underline}**
L49: 
L50: **Context.** Margin grids tied to leverage ratios are standard in middle-market revolving credit facilities. Key borrower concerns include: (a) the absolute level of the margin at each tier, (b) the number and spacing of tiers, and (c) the mechanics for adjusting the margin (quarterly reset upon delivery of compliance certificate is standard).
L51: 
L52: **3.1 Margin Levels**
L53: 
L54: **Preferred Position.** Margins should be benchmarked to recent comparable transactions. For a middle-market senior secured revolver with a strong credit profile (leverage under 3.50x), expect SOFR margins in the range of 175-300 bps across the grid. Opening margins at the lowest tier should target 175-200 bps; the highest tier should not exceed 300 bps.
L55: 
L56: **Acceptable Fallback.** An across-the-board increase of up to 12.5 bps per tier from the borrower\'s proposed grid may be acceptable in exchange for concessions on other terms (e.g., larger baskets, looser covenants). An increase of 25 bps across the board is at the outer edge of acceptability and should be treated as a significant pricing concession that requires commensurate value elsewhere in the documentation.
L57: 
L58: **Hard No.** An across-the-board increase exceeding 25 bps from the borrower\'s opening position without corresponding concessions on structural terms.
L59: 
L60: **3.2 Reset Mechanics**
L61: 
L62: **Preferred Position.** The applicable margin should be reset **quarterly** upon delivery of each compliance certificate. No restrictions on the frequency or direction of margin adjustments. If the borrower\'s leverage improves from one quarter to the next, the borrower should receive the benefit of a lower margin immediately.
L63: 
L64: **Acceptable Fallback.** Quarterly reset with a one-quarter lookback. A provision that sets the margin at the highest tier if a compliance certificate is not delivered on time is standard and acceptable.
L65: 
L66: **Hard No.** Any \"upward-only ratchet\" or \"annual-only downward adjustment\" mechanic. Such provisions are non-standard for revolving credit facilities and penalize the borrower for temporary leverage spikes. The margin must be permitted to adjust both upward and downward each quarter. An upward-only mechanic is appropriate only in severely distressed or workout situations and has no place in a performing middle-market credit facility.
L67: 
L68: **Negotiation Tips.** If the lender proposes both wider margins and a ratchet mechanic, address them as a package---agree to accept a modest margin increase (up to 12.5 bps) in exchange for deletion of the ratchet mechanic. Never concede both simultaneously.
L69: 
L70: **[Section 4: Financial Covenants --- Leverage Ratio and Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio]{.underline}**
L71: 
L72: **Context.** Financial covenants in middle-market revolving credit facilities typically include a maximum Total Leverage Ratio and a minimum Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio (FCCR), tested quarterly on a trailing four-quarter basis.
L73: 
L74: **4.1 Maximum Total Leverage Ratio**
L75: 
L76: **Preferred Position.** The opening leverage covenant at closing should provide at least 1.00x of headroom above the borrower\'s projected leverage at closing. Step-downs should occur no more frequently than annually and should be spaced to align with the borrower\'s projected deleveraging trajectory. Step-downs of 0.25x per step are standard; 0.50x per step is aggressive. The borrower should not agree to a terminal leverage covenant tighter than 0.50x above the borrower\'s projected leverage in the final year.
L77: 
L78: **Acceptable Fallback.** A step-down schedule accelerated by one year from the borrower\'s preferred schedule may be acceptable if the borrower\'s financial model demonstrates comfortable compliance (headroom of at least 0.50x). However, adding an *entirely new step-down level* not present in the original term sheet is more problematic and should be resisted.
L79: 
L80: **Hard No.** (i) Step-down acceleration of more than one year per step relative to the borrower\'s proposed schedule, (ii) addition of a terminal step-down that results in less than 0.50x of projected headroom, or (iii) step-downs that do not account for the borrower\'s stated acquisition strategy and associated temporary leverage increases.
L81: 
L82: **4.2 Minimum Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio**
L83: 
L84: **Preferred Position.** FCCR of 1.20x to 1.25x. This range provides adequate lender protection while giving the borrower reasonable operating flexibility.
L85: 
L86: **Acceptable Fallback.** FCCR up to 1.30x. A 1.30x FCCR is within market range for a well-performing middle-market borrower and provides a modest additional cushion for lenders.
L87: 
L88: **Hard No.** FCCR above 1.35x. At 1.35x or higher, the covenant begins to constrain the borrower\'s ability to invest in the business (capital expenditures and working capital are typically included in Fixed Charges). For sponsor-backed companies with active acquisition strategies, a 1.35x FCCR can quickly become a binding constraint in the quarters immediately following an acquisition.
L89: 
L90: **General Note.** When lenders propose tightening financial covenants, always evaluate the *cumulative* effect of all financial covenant changes. An accelerated step-down combined with a higher FCCR combined with a tighter EBITDA definition (see Section 5) can dramatically reduce covenant headroom even if each individual change seems modest.
L91: 
L92: **[Section 5: EBITDA Definition --- Add-Backs and Synergy Adjustments]{.underline}**
L93: 
L94: **Context.** The EBITDA definition is the single most consequential economic term in a credit agreement because it flows through to leverage calculations, coverage ratios, and basket sizes. Lenders routinely seek to constrain add-backs; borrowers (especially sponsor-backed companies pursuing acquisitions) need sufficient add-back capacity to reflect true earnings power.
L95: 
L96: **5.1 Non-Recurring / Extraordinary Add-Backs**
L97: 
L98: **Preferred Position.** Cap of \$5,000,000 per fiscal year with a lifetime cap of no less than \$15,000,000 (or 25% of EBITDA, whichever is greater). The per-year cap should accommodate restructuring, litigation settlement, and transaction-related non-recurring charges.
L99: 
L100: **Acceptable Fallback.** Cap of \$4,000,000 per fiscal year with a lifetime cap of \$12,000,000. Below this level, the cap becomes too restrictive for a middle-market company undergoing any integration or transformation activity.
L101: 
L102: **Hard No.** Per-year cap below \$3,500,000 or lifetime cap below \$10,000,000. Critically, if the lender proposes a lifetime cap that is simply the per-year cap multiplied by fewer than 4 years (e.g., \$3,000,000 × 3 = \$9,000,000 on a 5-year facility), this is a disguised tightening that should be rejected. The lifetime cap should be at least the per-year cap multiplied by the lesser of (x) the facility term in years or (y) 4.
L103: 
L104: **5.2 Cost Savings, Synergies, and Operating Expense Reductions (Acquisition-Related)**
L105: 
L106: **Preferred Position.** Synergy add-back capped at 15% of pro forma EBITDA, with a realization period of 18 months and a requirement that the borrower provide reasonable factual support for the projected synergies.
L107: 
L108: **Acceptable Fallback.** Synergy add-back capped at 12.5% of pro forma EBITDA, with a realization period of no less than 15 months. Below 12.5%, the cap becomes problematic for serial acquirers because the savings from even well-documented acquisitions often exceed 10% of pro forma EBITDA in the initial integration period.
L109: 
L110: **Hard No.** Synergy add-back below 10% of pro forma EBITDA, or a realization period shorter than 12 months. At 10% with a 12-month window, the borrower has the absolute minimum flexibility; below these thresholds, synergy add-backs become practically unusable for most mid-size acquisitions.
L111: 
L112: **Negotiation Tips.** EBITDA add-backs and synergy caps should be negotiated in tandem with permitted acquisition baskets (Section 6) and financial covenants (Section 4). If the lender tightens all three simultaneously, the compounding effect can be severe. Recommend conceding modestly on one category (e.g., accept a slightly lower per-year non-recurring cap) in exchange for maintaining the synergy cap and realization period.
L113: 
L114: **[Section 6: Permitted Acquisitions]{.underline}**
L115: 
L116: **Context.** For sponsor-backed borrowers pursuing a buy-and-build strategy, the permitted acquisition covenant is one of the most important provisions. Key variables include: individual and aggregate dollar caps, conditions to permitted acquisitions (pro forma compliance, no default, same business line, delivery of financial statements), and the rigor of pro forma compliance testing.
L117: 
L118: **6.1 Size Baskets**
L119: 
L120: **Preferred Position.** Individual acquisition cap of \$25,000,000 or greater; annual aggregate cap of \$60,000,000 or greater. These levels accommodate typical tuck-in acquisitions in the lower-middle-market infrastructure services space.
L121: 
L122: **Acceptable Fallback.** Individual acquisition cap of \$20,000,000; annual aggregate cap of \$50,000,000. Below these thresholds, the borrower may be unable to execute its pipeline without seeking lender consent (which introduces delay, uncertainty, and potential for lender holdout).
L123: 
L124: **Hard No.** Individual cap below \$15,000,000 or annual aggregate below \$40,000,000. At \$15,000,000/\$40,000,000, the baskets are too small for a borrower of this size and effectively require consent for any meaningful acquisition.
L125: 
L126: **6.2 Pro Forma Compliance Testing**
L127: 
L128: **Preferred Position.** Pro forma compliance tested against the financial covenants *as in effect at the time of the acquisition* (no pro forma cushion). This is market-standard for middle-market revolving credit facilities.
L129: 
L130: **Acceptable Fallback.** Pro forma compliance tested at financial covenants 0.10x-0.15x tighter than those in effect. A modest cushion is sometimes accepted in exchange for larger baskets.
L131: 
L132: **Hard No.** Pro forma cushion of 0.25x or more. A 0.25x cushion is effectively double-counting the covenant protection (the borrower must already comply with the covenant at the as-in-effect level; adding 0.25x is equivalent to running the covenant at a tighter level entirely). For example, if the leverage covenant is 3.50x and the pro forma cushion is 0.25x, the borrower must demonstrate compliance at 3.25x---which may be tighter than the covenant level *scheduled for the following year*. This creates an absurd result where the borrower must pre-comply with future covenants for current acquisitions.
L133: 
L134: **Negotiation Tips.** If the lender insists on a pro forma cushion, push for it to be no greater than 0.10x and negotiate that it applies only to the leverage ratio, not to the FCCR. Alternatively, propose that the pro forma cushion applies only to acquisitions above a specified threshold (e.g., acquisitions with enterprise value exceeding \$10,000,000), exempting smaller tuck-in transactions.
L135: 
L136: **[Section 7: Restricted Payments / Distributions to Equity Holders]{.underline}**
L137: 
L138: **Context.** Restricted payment covenants directly affect the sponsor\'s ability to extract returns from the portfolio company. For sponsor-backed borrowers, maintaining distribution flexibility is typically a high priority. The key variables are: (a) whether there is a hard dollar cap, (b) the pro forma leverage test, and (c) any ECF-based limitation.
L139: 
L140: **Preferred Position.** No hard dollar cap on restricted payments. Distributions permitted subject to (i) no default or event of default, (ii) pro forma compliance with the Total Leverage Ratio at 3.00x or the then-applicable covenant level (whichever is greater), and (iii) an annual aggregate limitation tied to a percentage of Excess Cash Flow (e.g., 50% of ECF). The absence of a hard dollar cap allows the sponsor to receive distributions proportionate to the company\'s performance.
L141: 
L142: **Acceptable Fallback.** Hard dollar cap of no less than the greater of \$12,000,000 and 20% of EBITDA, combined with a pro forma leverage test at no tighter than 2.75x. Below \$12,000,000, the cap becomes a meaningful constraint for a sponsor expecting annual distributions.
L143: 
L144: **Hard No.** (i) Hard dollar cap below \$10,000,000 per fiscal year, (ii) pro forma leverage test tighter than 2.50x, or (iii) the combination of a hard dollar cap AND a tighter leverage test AND an ECF sweep (see Section 11), which together can effectively eliminate distributions in any year where the borrower is actively acquiring. The cumulative effect of all three restrictions must be evaluated together.
L145: 
L146: **Negotiation Tips.** If the lender insists on a hard cap, negotiate for a \"grower\" basket that increases with EBITDA (e.g., the greater of \$12,000,000 and 20% of trailing four-quarter EBITDA). If the lender tightens the leverage test, push for the no-cap structure. Do not concede both simultaneously. Remind the lender that restricting distributions excessively can trigger co-investment and management retention issues for the sponsor, which is not in the lender\'s long-term interest.
L147: 
L148: **[Section 8: Events of Default --- Cross-Default Threshold]{.underline}**
L149: 
L150: **Context.** The cross-default threshold determines at what dollar level a default on other indebtedness triggers a default under the revolving credit facility. Setting this threshold appropriately prevents immaterial disputes from cascading into a facility-wide default.
L151: 
L152: **Preferred Position.** Cross-default threshold of \$5,000,000 or higher. For a borrower with total debt in excess of \$60,000,000 and EBITDA approaching \$60,000,000, a \$5,000,000 threshold (approximately 8-9% of total debt) is proportionate and market-standard.
L153: 
L154: **Acceptable Fallback.** Cross-default threshold of \$3,500,000. Below \$3,500,000, the threshold begins to capture disputes on individual equipment leases, vendor financing arrangements, and other immaterial obligations.
L155: 
L156: **Hard No.** Cross-default threshold below \$2,500,000. At levels below \$2,500,000, the threshold is disproportionately low for a borrower of this size and can be triggered by routine commercial disputes on small-dollar obligations. For borrowers with significant equipment financing portfolios (common in infrastructure services), individual lease obligations may be in the \$250,000-\$500,000 range, and aggregation to a \$1,000,000 threshold is trivially easy.
L157: 
L158: **Negotiation Tips.** If the lender pushes for a lower threshold, propose qualifiers: (a) exclude bona fide disputes being contested in good faith, (b) require the default on the third-party obligation to have actually been declared (not merely that an event has occurred that with notice or lapse of time could become a default), and (c) exclude obligations below \$500,000 individually from the aggregation calculation.
L159: 
L160: **[Section 9: Change of Control]{.underline}**
L161: 
L162: **Context.** Change of control provisions define when a change in the equity ownership or governance of the borrower triggers a mandatory prepayment, default, or other remedial event. For sponsor-backed companies, the threshold at which the sponsor\'s ownership stake triggers a change of control is a critical negotiation point.
L163: 
L164: **Preferred Position.** Change of control triggered only when the sponsor ceases to hold at least 35% of the equity interests in the borrower (on a fully diluted basis). A 35% threshold accommodates standard sponsor exit mechanisms including management equity programs (typically 10-15% of equity), co-investor allocations, secondary sales of minority interests, and IPO-related dilution. At 35%, the sponsor retains effective control through board representation and contractual governance rights even after significant dilution.
L165: 
L166: **Acceptable Fallback.** Change of control triggered at 40% equity ownership. A 40% threshold is workable for most sponsor structures, though it limits flexibility for management equity pools and co-investments.
L167: 
L168: **Hard No.** Change of control triggered at a threshold above 45%. A 50% or 51% threshold is unacceptable for sponsor-backed borrowers because: (i) management equity incentive plans routinely dilute the sponsor to 55-65% within 2-3 years of acquisition, (ii) co-investors and independent board members may hold 5-10% collectively, and (iii) the sponsor would need to maintain a supermajority stake to avoid triggering a change of control, which conflicts with normal-course portfolio management. A 51% threshold essentially prevents any meaningful equity dilution and is inconsistent with market practice for leveraged finance transactions.
L169: 
L170: **Negotiation Tips.** If the lender is concerned about the sponsor abandoning the investment, propose supplemental protections: (a) require the sponsor to maintain board control (majority of board seats or appointment of the CEO) as an alternative to a pure equity percentage test, (b) add a \"passive investor\" carve-out so that issuances of non-voting equity do not dilute the sponsor\'s percentage, and (c) clarify that the equity test is measured on a fully diluted basis to include all outstanding warrants, options, and convertible instruments.
L171: 
L172: **[Section 10: Anti-Layering, Most-Favored-Nation, and Debt Incurrence Protections]{.underline}**
L173: 
L174: **Context.** Lenders frequently seek protections against the borrower incurring additional debt that could dilute the lender\'s recovery or provide better economic terms to other creditors. Two common provisions are (a) anti-layering covenants (preventing structurally or contractually senior debt) and (b) MFN (most-favored-nation) clauses (requiring the existing facility\'s terms to match or nearly match better terms offered to future creditors).
L175: 
L176: **10.1 Anti-Layering. Preferred Position.** Accept a standard anti-layering covenant prohibiting the borrower from incurring debt that is structurally or contractually senior to the revolving credit facility obligations. This is market-standard and appropriate for senior secured revolving facilities.
L177: 
L178: **Acceptable Fallback.** Same as Preferred Position. Anti-layering is standard; no fallback needed.
L179: 
L180: **Hard No.** None. Anti-layering is a reasonable and expected protection.
L181: 
L182: **10.2 Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) Clause**
L183: 
L184: **Preferred Position.** No MFN clause. MFN clauses are appropriate in certain first-lien/second-lien intercreditor contexts and in broadly syndicated term loan B facilities but are not standard in middle-market revolving credit facilities.
L185: 
L186: **Acceptable Fallback.** If the lender insists on an MFN, it should apply **only to pari passu senior secured facilities** (i.e., facilities sharing the same collateral pool on a first-priority basis). The MFN must expressly exclude: (i) subordinated debt, (ii) mezzanine debt, (iii) second-lien debt, (iv) unsecured debt, (v) permitted equipment financing and purchase money debt, and (vi) any debt that is junior in right of payment or collateral priority. The MFN cushion should be at least 75 bps (not 50 bps). A sunset of 12-18 months after closing is also advisable.
L187: 
L188: **Hard No.** An MFN clause that applies to all future indebtedness including subordinated, mezzanine, or second-lien debt. Such a provision effectively prevents the borrower from accessing the subordinated debt market at any price higher than the revolving facility margin plus the MFN cushion, because the entire revolving facility would be repriced upward. This is economically irrational---subordinated debt is inherently more expensive than senior secured debt, and an MFN triggered by sub debt reprices the senior facility without any change in the senior lender\'s risk profile. Additionally, an MFN cushion of less than 75 bps for pari passu facilities is too tight and should be resisted.
L189: 
L190: **Negotiation Tips.** If the lender proposes a broad MFN, ask for the commercial rationale. The lender\'s legitimate concern is that the borrower does not create a pari passu facility at a higher margin that disadvantages the existing lender. Address that concern narrowly---limit the MFN to pari passu first-lien facilities and exclude everything else. If the lender will not accept a carve-out for sub debt, propose a \"yield protection\" formulation that applies only if the pari passu margin exceeds the existing margin by more than 100 bps.
L191: 
L192: **[Section 11: Mandatory Prepayment --- Excess Cash Flow Sweeps]{.underline}**
L193: 
L194: **Context.** Excess Cash Flow (ECF) sweeps require the borrower to use a percentage of its annual ECF to prepay outstanding loans. ECF sweeps are standard features of **term loans** in leveraged finance. Their inclusion in revolving credit facilities is **atypical and should be questioned.**
L195: 
L196: **11.1 ECF Sweeps in Revolving Credit Facilities**
L197: 
L198: **Preferred Position.** No ECF sweep in a revolving credit facility. ECF sweeps are designed for amortizing term loans where the lender does not have the benefit of mandatory commitment reductions. In a revolving facility, the lender\'s exposure is protected by the borrower\'s obligation to repay drawn amounts and by the lender\'s ability to decline future borrowing requests after a default. Requiring ECF-based mandatory prepayment on a revolving facility creates an artificial cash drain that undermines the revolving facility\'s core purpose---providing flexible liquidity.
L199: 
L200: **Acceptable Fallback.** If an ECF sweep is included (often at the insistence of lenders in more leveraged credits), it must include leverage-based step-downs. Market-standard structure: 50% sweep at Total Leverage Ratio \> 3.00x, stepping down to 25% at ≤ 3.00x but \> 2.50x, and 0% at ≤ 2.50x. The step-downs reward the borrower for deleveraging and are a fundamental component of ECF mechanics in leveraged finance.
L201: 
L202: **Hard No.** (i) Any ECF sweep in a revolving credit facility without step-downs, and (ii) any ECF sweep set at a flat percentage regardless of leverage. A flat 50% sweep at all leverage levels is punitive and inconsistent with market practice even for term loans. If the borrower has deleveraged to below 2.50x, there is no economic justification for sweeping 50% of ECF---the lender\'s credit risk has materially improved and the borrower should retain cash for reinvestment. Eliminating step-downs is a significant departure from market norms and should be treated as a red-flag provision.
L203: 
L204: **Negotiation Tips.** If the lender insists on an ECF sweep in the revolver, propose that it apply only during periods when Total Leverage exceeds 3.00x (i.e., a single-tier sweep with a 0% rate at all lower leverage levels). Alternatively, propose that ECF prepayments reduce future revolving commitments rather than require cash prepayment, which at least preserves the borrower\'s liquidity position. Also consider proposing a de minimis threshold for ECF (e.g., no sweep if ECF is below \$5,000,000 in a fiscal year).
L205: 
L206: **Market Context Note for Attorneys.** Practitioners should be aware that the inclusion of ECF sweeps in revolving credit facilities has been raised in certain club deals since 2023, particularly by regional banks with conservative credit cultures. However, it remains non-standard in LMA and LSTA-template-based documentation, and most sophisticated borrowers successfully push back against it.
L207: 
L208: **[Section 12: Material Adverse Effect (MAE) Definition]{.underline}**
L209: 
L210: **Context.** The MAE definition is used throughout the credit agreement in representations, conditions precedent, and events of default. The precision of the MAE definition determines the lender\'s ability to declare a default or refuse to fund based on an adverse development in the borrower\'s business.
L211: 
L212: **Preferred Position.** The MAE definition should include the following critical qualifiers:
L213: 
L214: > \(a\) The standard \"business, operations, property, assets, liabilities, or financial condition **of the Borrower and its Subsidiaries, taken as a whole**\" language. The \"taken as a whole\" qualifier ensures that an adverse change affecting one subsidiary or one business line does not constitute an MAE unless it is material at the enterprise level.
L215: >
L216: > \(b\) The word \"material\" should appear in each sub-clause that describes a category of adverse change.
L217: >
L218: > \(c\) Standard carve-outs for (i) changes in general economic or market conditions, (ii) changes in the borrower\'s industry generally, (iii) changes in law or accounting standards, (iv) effects of the transactions contemplated by the credit agreement, and (v) changes resulting from force majeure events or natural disasters---in each case, unless such changes disproportionately affect the borrower relative to other participants in the same industry.
L219: 
L220: **Acceptable Fallback.** Minor variations in the carve-out language are acceptable. Removal of one or two sub-clauses of carve-outs may be acceptable if the remaining carve-outs are sufficiently broad. However, the core \"taken as a whole\" language and the word \"material\" in each sub-clause are not negotiable.
L221: 
L222: **Hard No.** (i) Removal of the \"taken as a whole\" qualifier. Under New York law and Delaware law (the most common governing law choices for credit agreements), the \"taken as a whole\" standard is a foundational element of MAE jurisprudence. Its removal would allow the lender to assert an MAE based on an adverse change in a single subsidiary or a single contract, even if the borrower\'s consolidated business remains healthy. This is inconsistent with established case law precedent requiring enterprise-level assessment. (ii) Removal of the word \"material\" from sub-clauses. Without \"material,\" any adverse change---however trivial---could theoretically satisfy the MAE trigger. This inverts the purpose of the MAE clause, which is to capture only *material* adverse developments.
L223: 
L224: **Negotiation Tips.** The \"taken as a whole\" language is so fundamental that its removal should be treated as a drafting error or a sign that the lender\'s counsel is overreaching. If opposing counsel removes it, request a brief call to discuss---it is often restored quickly once the issue is raised directly. If the lender\'s counsel refuses to restore it, escalate to the lead partner for a commercial discussion with the relationship manager. Do not accept a credit agreement without \"taken as a whole\" in the MAE definition.
L225: 
L226: **[Section 13: Asset Sale Provisions and Reinvestment Periods]{.underline}**
L227: 
L228: **Note.** This section provides brief guidance relevant to mandatory prepayment mechanics triggered by asset sales but does not directly address reinvestment period length in the full three-tier framework. Attorneys should cross-reference Section 11 (Mandatory Prepayment) for related guidance.
L229: 
L230: **Asset Sale Mandatory Prepayment.** Mandatory prepayment from asset sale net proceeds is standard, with a per-transaction threshold of \$2,500,000-\$5,000,000 being market-standard for middle-market facilities.
L231: 
L232: **Reinvestment Period.** The playbook does not set specific Preferred/Fallback/Hard No positions for reinvestment periods. However, as a general matter, the firm notes that reinvestment periods shorter than 270 days may be operationally challenging for borrowers in capital-intensive industries (infrastructure, construction, manufacturing) where replacement asset procurement and deployment cycles commonly require 9-12 months. Attorneys should evaluate the appropriateness of any proposed reinvestment period against the borrower\'s specific operational cadence.
L233: 
L234: **[Section 14: General Negotiation Principles and Tactical Guidance]{.underline}**
L235: 
L236: **Prioritization Framework.** When facing a markup with numerous adverse changes, attorneys should categorize deviations into three tiers: (1) provisions that must be restored to the original or near-original position (Hard No items), (2) provisions that should be improved but where compromise is acceptable (Acceptable Fallback items), and (3) provisions where the markup is within market range and can be conceded strategically (to build goodwill and demonstrate reasonableness). Always identify at least 2-3 provisions to concede voluntarily---this establishes credibility and makes it harder for the lender\'s counsel to characterize the borrower as obstructionist.
L237: 
L238: **Cumulative Impact Analysis.** Never evaluate individual provision changes in isolation. The interaction between financial covenant tightening, EBITDA definition restrictions, acquisition basket reductions, distribution constraints, and ECF sweeps can create a \"covenant vise\" that leaves the borrower with minimal headroom. Always model the cumulative impact using the borrower\'s financial projections before finalizing the negotiation response.
L239: 
L240: **Timing Leverage.** When the borrower has a hard closing deadline (e.g., refinancing an existing maturity, acquisition closing date), the lender has inherent leverage. Disclose deadlines strategically---acknowledge urgency without appearing desperate. Where possible, maintain optionality by keeping alternative financing discussions alive (even informally) until the credit agreement is executed.
L241: 
L242: **Documentation of Concessions.** Track all concessions made and received during the negotiation in a concession log. This log supports the lead partner in discussions with the client and the sponsor about the overall quality of the negotiated deal.
L243: 
L244: **Escalation Protocol.** If the lender\'s markup contains more than three Hard No items (per this playbook), the senior associate should immediately bring the markup to the lead partner\'s attention and prepare a briefing memo before scheduling the first negotiation call. Multiple Hard No items may indicate a fundamental mismatch in credit appetite, and the client may wish to explore alternative lenders.
